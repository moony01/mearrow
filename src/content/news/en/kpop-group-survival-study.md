Debut night is the image K-pop sells best: a stage, a name, and the promise that everything starts now. A census of 1,182 groups suggests the less visible test comes next. A study published in July in the *Journal of Korea Entertainment Industry Association* measured an average group activity period of 4.12 years. Only 55.03% were still active three years after debut. That means 44.97% were no longer active at that point; it does **not** mean every one of those groups formally announced a disbandment.

The research covered groups debuting from 1996 through 2025, the era beginning with H.O.T. Its record is available through [KCI](https://kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART003366423), while [Newsis](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260804_0003735284?pc_view=1) and [The Asia Business Daily](https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2026080516272694487) reported the findings. For aspiring performers, the number is a hard market signal—not a forecast for any one trainee or team.

## A 30-year map beyond the headline acts

### The cohort is large, but the unit is still the group

Professor Kim Jeong-seop analyzed 1,182 idol groups that debuted over three decades: 588 girl groups and 594 boy groups. The paper combines statistical and cohort analysis with expert interviews. Its scale gives readers a wider view than a single agency or debut season can provide, though it does not turn group averages into personal odds.

The study also describes how concentrated commercial hits are. Reports by [Newsis](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260804_0003735284?pc_view=1) and [Edaily](https://en.edaily.co.kr/news/eda202608185590/) say 42 groups, or 3.55% of the sample, sold more than 300,000 copies of a single album; 19 groups, or 1.61%, passed one million. Those are useful measures of how rare the largest sales milestones were in the dataset. They are not audited profit figures for every company, and album sales alone cannot show whether a group was financially sustainable.

That distinction matters for trainees. A visible debut or a strong first-week chart position can attract attention, but neither alone proves that an agency has the budget and plan to keep a group active over multiple years.

## The survival drop arrives before year seven

### A contract benchmark and an activity average measure different things

The cohort figures reported by [The Asia Business Daily](https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2026080516272694487) show the survival rate falling from 87.99% one year after debut to 55.03% at year three, 36.62% at year five, 26.14% at year seven, and 17.92% at year ten. The average activity period of 4.12 years equals 58.9% of a seven-year comparison point cited in coverage of the paper.

That seven-year figure needs careful handling. South Korea's [standard singer exclusive-contract form](https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/flDownload.do?bylClsCd=200201&flSeq=140964437), revised by the Culture Ministry in 2024, says the contract term under the template cannot exceed seven years unless both sides agree in writing to an extension. It is a standard-form contract term, not a clock that guarantees every group will perform for seven years. The study measures reported group activity, not the expiration dates of every member's contract.

In [Newsis's report on the study](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260804_0003735284?pc_view=1), the author points to the first one to three years as an investment decision window: early results in music and audience growth can shape whether a company continues investing from year four onward. This is a proposed explanation for the curve, not proof that one streaming milestone or album number causes a group to stop.

## The gender gap is real; the explanation needs care

### Different averages do not decide an individual group's future

The study reports average activity periods of 5.11 years for boy groups and 3.13 years for girl groups, a difference of 1.98 years, as [Newsis](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260804_0003735284?pc_view=1) also reports. In coverage of the study, the researcher connects that gap to different revenue patterns: recurring purchases by loyal fandoms on one side, and greater reliance on general-public recognition, streaming, and events on the other.

That interpretation points to a business question, not a rule about which groups can last. An average does not account for every group's agency, release schedule, member lineup, audience, or international strategy. The figures do not show that a girl group is destined to end earlier, or that any single fandom model guarantees long-term activity. Averages describe a market pattern; they do not grade a trainee's ability or potential.

## For trainees, debut is the start of a business test

### Ask what support exists after the showcase

The sharpest lesson is not “most debuts fail.” It is that debut does not settle whether a group can keep working. The first three years sit between the training system and a durable audience: a company has to keep producing music and performances while finding repeat listeners and fans willing to stay engaged.

That makes the agency's plan as important as the trainee's skill. Before signing or debuting, aspiring performers and families can ask how releases and promotion are planned, who pays which costs, what support is available for school and health, how the group will reach an audience, and what happens if the first concept needs to change. These questions do not guarantee a long career, but they make the path less dependent on promises made on debut day.

The contract comparison is also worth reading alongside the industry's [standard-form trainee contract changes](/studio/news/kpop-trainee-contract-reform-2026-foreign-gaps), while remembering that a standard form and an individual agreement are not the same document. A trainee's route and protections can vary by age, agency, and signed terms.

## MEARROW's read: look for a runway, not a miracle

### Early attention is not the same as durable support

MEARROW reads the census as a reminder to separate three milestones that often blur together: getting selected, making a debut, and building a group that can continue. The 4.12-year average describes the final one. The first two can look dramatic online, but they do not reveal how much production capacity, funding, staff time, or member care is available after the launch campaign ends.

For a small agency, the useful question is not whether it can promise a hit. It is whether it can build repeatable operations: a realistic release cadence, a clear audience, a manageable roster, and support that does not disappear after the first single. MEARROW has covered one attempt to widen that runway through [support for smaller K-pop agencies](/studio/news/kpop-indie-agency-fund). A program can improve access to resources; it cannot guarantee that a particular group will survive.

For fans, the same lens makes support more concrete. A group can be active without being a blockbuster, and commercial success for one release does not tell the full story of members' working conditions. Consistent releases, sustainable schedules, communication, and long-term audience connection are better questions than asking every rookie group to become an instant million-seller.

## What the census cannot tell one trainee

### Activity is not the same as a formal ending

This is a group-level study of a 30-year industry, not a database of every trainee's personal outcome. Its average cannot calculate one person's chance of debuting, predict a specific team's future, or explain every decision an agency makes. The study's activity measure also should not be rewritten as a formal disbandment count: a group may pause, change its lineup, or stop regular releases without a single announcement that captures every member's next step.

The number is still useful. It shows why trainees should look beyond the debut showcase and why agencies need more than launch-day attention to build lasting acts. A career can take many paths; the data says the support plan after debut deserves the same scrutiny as the audition that came before it.
