To become a K-pop trainee, you move to Korea — that rule ends on March 31. HYBE is launching simultaneous large-scale auditions across 10 Indian cities: Ahmedabad, Bengaluru, Chandigarh, Chennai, Delhi, Guwahati, Hyderabad, Kolkata, Mumbai, and Pune. Add London, New York, Singapore, Toronto, and Sydney rounds for overseas Indians, and that's 15 cities total.

This is the largest audition ever held by a single K-pop agency in a single country. And when you see who's bankrolling it, you realize this isn't just about finding trainees.

## Why India, Why Now

### The Weight of 1.4 Billion

India's population: 1.4 billion. Median age: roughly 29. It's the world's youngest major market, with an overwhelming concentration of people in K-pop's core demographic of teens and twenties. Yet the K-pop industry has never seriously targeted India. Japan, China, Southeast Asia, the U.S., Europe — that was the order. India was always "next."

HYBE just turned "next" into "now." According to The Korea Herald, this audition is the key test case for Chairman Bang Si-hyuk's **"multi-home, multi-genre"** strategy — a plan to transplant K-pop's production methodology into other regions and create entirely new intellectual property.

### A Proven Formula

HYBE has already run this experiment twice. First in Japan: **&TEAM**, born through HYBE JAPAN, became a million-seller in both Korea and Japan. Then in the U.S.: **KATSEYE**, created with Geffen Records, hit the Billboard Hot 100 and Billboard 200 within a year of debut and earned a Grammy nomination.

Critically, KATSEYE includes **Lara Raj**, a member of Indian descent. An Indian-origin artist in a global K-pop group is proof of concept — proof that K-pop stars can come from India.

## The '360-Degree System' — What HYBE Is Really Transplanting

### Exporting K-Pop Methodology

HYBE India defined the audition's goal as: **"Establishing itself as a cultural hub in South Asia and locally applying HYBE's 360-degree music industry capabilities — discovering, producing, marketing, and performing talent."**

Breaking that down:
- **Discovering**: In-person auditions across 10 cities + online submissions
- **Producing**: World-class Training & Development (T&D) programs for selected candidates
- **Marketing**: Leveraging local digital platforms like Snapchat and BookMyShow
- **Performing**: Potential for debut and activities based in India

The crucial point: **selected artists may not need to move to Korea at all.** The essence of the multi-home strategy isn't "make in Korea, export everywhere" — it's **"create locally, launch locally."** Just as &TEAM operates in Japan performing in Japanese, artists from India could debut in India performing in Hindi or English.

## MEARROW's Take: Is K-Pop Still 'Korean'?

### How Far Does the 'K' Extend?

Bang Si-hyuk's India audition poses a fundamental question: **Does the 'K' in K-pop stand for Korea — or for a methodology?**

Until now, K-pop was made in Korea, in Korean, with Korean artists at the center. Yes, there were foreign members — TWICE's Sana and Momo (Japan), GOT7's Jackson (Hong Kong), BLACKPINK's Lisa (Thailand). But every one of them **moved to Korea, trained within the Korean system, and debuted in Korean.**

HYBE's multi-home strategy flips that premise. If you can recruit Indians in India, train them in India, and debut them in Hindi or English — is that still K-pop?

MEARROW's answer: **"K-pop is not a genre — it's a system."** Systematic trainee development, precision visual planning, global fandom building — if this methodology is K-pop's essence, then it's K-pop regardless of where it's made. Bang Si-hyuk is exporting the system itself.

## India's Next K-Pop Star

On March 31, the people lining up at audition venues across India will mostly be teenagers and young adults in their early twenties. A generation that grew up watching BTS, dancing to BLACKPINK, and believing "I can do it too" after seeing KATSEYE's Lara Raj.

What HYBE is bringing to India isn't an audition. It's the entire K-pop system. And the moment that system takes root in a nation of 1.4 billion, the definition of K-pop changes forever.

That moment begins on March 31.
