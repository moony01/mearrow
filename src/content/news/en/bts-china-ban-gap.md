As BTS’ tour map lit up with Seoul, Los Angeles, London and Sao Paulo, one blank space stood out more than any other: mainland China. That absence is not just a routing choice. When a group returning from a nearly four-year hiatus can mount a 12-month global run and still leave China off the board, it says something larger about the `BTS China` question and the unresolved state of the `K-pop China market`. This comeback is not only a celebration. It is also a reminder that K-pop’s biggest unfinished market story still has not been resolved.

## Why the China gap looks bigger this time

### A bigger tour makes the missing stop more obvious

In its April 10 analysis, AP focused directly on the fact that mainland China was absent from BTS’ new world tour. The point was not that the omission was shocking on its own. The point was that adding China would have been the real surprise. That is how normalized the gap has become. But scale changes perception. Korea JoongAng Daily reported in February that analysts expected BTS’ tour to stretch to roughly 80 performances and draw millions of concertgoers. Once the tour becomes that large, the missing stop stops looking incidental and starts looking structural.

### Fans are already paying the cost across borders

One of the sharpest details in AP’s report was that Chinese fans were expected to fly to Seoul for the opening shows. That is the real cost of the gap. The loss is not only in unsold local tickets. Airlines, hotels, merchandise and city-level spending all move somewhere else. Demand does not disappear when mainland access is blocked; it leaks outward. That is why this `K-pop China market` story matters beyond fandom frustration. It is about one of the industry’s biggest spending pools being redirected to other cities.

## China looks warmer, but mainland access is still another matter

### The shadow of the 2016 restrictions has not fully lifted

This did not begin yesterday. AP and The Korea Times both trace the long freeze to the backlash over South Korea’s 2016 THAAD deployment. Beijing has never formally acknowledged a hallyu ban, but the practical effect on concerts and Korean entertainment imports has been clear for years. There have been signs of thawing. The Korea Times reported in January and May that offline promotions were returning, K-pop albums were reappearing more visibly in Chinese retail channels, and some in the industry were once again talking about large-scale shows. Still, signals of improvement are not the same thing as stable approval.

### Hong Kong and Macau are open, but mainland approval is different

That is why so many K-pop acts still route through Hong Kong and Macau. They offer access to Greater China demand without fully depending on mainland approvals. The Korea Times recently described how K-pop performances have concentrated there while mainland restrictions remain uncertain. AP framed BTS’ mainland absence in the same broader reality. Even the case that once looked like a breakthrough — EPEX’s planned Fuzhou concert — ended in postponement, underlining just how fragile the situation remains. ## The numbers already show why China still matters

### Demand is not gone; access is what remains incomplete

It is only half true to say China is no longer what it once was for K-pop. The Korea Times, citing Korea Customs data, reported that album exports to China jumped from $4.31 million to $17.63 million in the first four months of 2025. Yonhap also reported that total K-pop album exports topped $300 million in 2025 for the first time, with China reclaiming the No. 2 spot at $69.7 million. In other words, the fandom demand is still there. What remains broken is the route into the market. That is why a BTS-scale tour leaving mainland China blank immediately reads as an industry signal, not just a scheduling detail.

## How MEARROW reads the BTS China gap

### The group is back, but market trust is not

MEARROW does not see this as a simple omission on BTS’ calendar. It looks more like a quiet admission that K-pop has learned how to grow without full China access, but still has not truly replaced what China can mean at scale. The U.S. brings prestige. Japan brings consistency. China, when it opens, can expand touring, album sales, platform deals and brand partnerships at the same time. The real question now is not whether China eventually reopens. It is which artists will be allowed back first, and under what conditions.

## What the next phase depends on

### Better headlines will not change the tour map

The tone in 2026 is clearly different from the last few years. More promotions are happening on the ground, and the political language around cultural exchange sounds less rigid. But as BTS’ tour makes clear, real change starts with approvals, not optimism. The `BTS China` gap tells the industry something blunt: K-pop still has not fully recovered its biggest unfinished market, and that empty slot is more expensive than the business likes to admit.
