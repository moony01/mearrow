On July 24, 1896, American ethnologist Alice C. Fletcher set up a phonograph at Howard University in Washington, D.C. She recorded three Korean students — Ahn Jeong-sik, Lee Hee-Cheol, and Son Rong — singing a Korean folk song called Arirang. It was the first time Korean voices had ever been captured on recording equipment. The Library of Congress holds the cylinder to this day.

Those students were at Howard for a reason: white universities wouldn't accept them.

One hundred and thirty years later, BTS centered their fifth studio album on that exact history. The album is called "ARIRANG." The animated teaser recreates the 1896 recording scene at Howard — the campus, Founders Library, Korean students performing. And in that animated scene at one of America's most historically Black institutions, most of the crowd watching is depicted as white.

The backlash in the United States has been significant.

## Why Arirang Was Recorded at Howard

The story of how Arirang came to be recorded at an HBCU is not widely known outside academic circles, even among K-pop fans. In 1896, the United States operated under formal racial segregation. Korean students arriving from Joseon-era Korea couldn't enter white academic establishments. Howard University — founded in 1867, in the immediate aftermath of the Civil War, to educate freed slaves and their descendants — was one of the few institutions that would take them.

That is the context in which Alice Fletcher recorded those voices. A university built by and for Black Americans, in a segregated country, became the site of the first Korean musical recording. The irony of American racism creating this specific historical convergence is exactly the kind of story BTS was leaning into.

## What the Animation Actually Shows

The teaser was structured as a historical narrative — tracing BTS's debut back through 2013, through the folk song's history, to the 1896 Howard recording. HYBE included a disclaimer in the video noting it was a "modern reimagining" that might "deviate from historical events."

The problem, critics argue, is which direction the "reimagining" went. The animation placed BTS's Korean students in front of a crowd at Howard University — and that crowd was predominantly white. The historical Black student community that defined Howard's campus was effectively erased from the scene.

Howard University's student newspaper, *The Hilltop*, captured the mixed reaction on campus. One student said she was excited to see Howard represented internationally, but that the whitewashing was "hurtful." Another put it more directly: "If they're going to use our campus and our history as the backdrop for their story, at least get it right."

The reaction outside campus was sharper. Reddit's r/kpopnoir thread — which draws primarily Black K-pop fans — lit up with thousands of responses. "So BTS decided to whitewash Howard University in their trailer… yeah I'm done." The r/Fauxmoi thread framing was blunt: "The fact that HYBE has remained silent speaks volumes on how little they care."

## HYBE's Silence

As of publication, HYBE has issued no apology and no detailed explanation.

The critics' central argument isn't just historical inaccuracy — HYBE's own disclaimer acknowledged that. The argument is about the direction of the inaccuracy. In a "modern reimagining," the choice was to depict the audience at a historically Black campus as predominantly white. That choice wasn't random. It reflects something about whose presence is considered visually unremarkable and whose is considered worthy of deliberate inclusion.

The irony compounds when you consider what the album is about. BTS chose "Arirang" as their subject because the song represents Korean identity, perseverance, and cultural continuity. The entire premise rests on Howard University being meaningful — on the fact that those seven Korean students in 1896 found refuge at a Black institution when the rest of America rejected them. To then visually erase the Black community from that space undermines the very foundation the story is built on.

## K-Pop's Long, Unresolved Debt to Black Culture

This controversy doesn't exist in isolation. It surfaces a structural problem the K-pop industry has never adequately addressed.

K-pop owes substantial creative debts to Black American music — in rhythm, in dance vocabulary, in visual aesthetics. The genre has absorbed these influences while repeatedly failing to properly credit or respect the culture they came from. There have been blackface incidents, racist remarks, cultural appropriation of Black hairstyles, and a general pattern of treating Black aesthetics as freely available raw material.

This specific case is different in form but not in kind. It's not overt mockery. It's something quieter and more systemic: using a Black historical space as the backdrop for a story about Korean identity, while visually erasing the Black people from that space. It's erasure by omission rather than by insult — which makes it, in some ways, harder to confront directly.

## MEARROW's Take

BTS's decision to build an album around the 1896 Howard recording was genuinely ambitious and historically interesting. The history deserves attention. The connection between Korean diasporic music and America's HBCU landscape is a story worth telling.

But telling that story while removing Black people from their own campus is not just a minor visual oversight. It reflects a worldview about who gets to be a passive backdrop to someone else's narrative.

**Howard exists because Black Americans built it.** The Korean students were welcomed there because that institution stood against racial exclusion. To animate that scene without the Black community that made the space possible is to tell a version of history in which the people who enabled it become invisible.

HYBE's silence makes it worse. A prompt acknowledgment — even a partial one — would have demonstrated some awareness of what was depicted. Instead: nothing.

Whether this becomes a sustained controversy or fades in the news cycle depends largely on whether HYBE responds. But what it has already revealed is a familiar pattern: K-pop reaching into Black cultural spaces for historical weight and artistic credibility, while failing to hold that history with the care it demands.
