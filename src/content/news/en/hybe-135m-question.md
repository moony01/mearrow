`Bang Si-hyuk warrant` is the kind of phrase HYBE never wanted sitting next to a BTS comeback cycle. Yet that is where the company now is. Prosecutors have rejected the police request to arrest HYBE's chairman, but the rejection did not erase the investigation. It only changed the shape of the story: Bang avoided immediate detention, while the market, fans and regulators are still left with a much larger question about how one of K-pop's most valuable companies handled its pre-IPO past. ## The Warrant Rejection Was Not A Full Stop

### Prosecutors blocked detention, not the investigation itself

On `April 24`, the Seoul Southern District Prosecutors' Office turned down a police request for an arrest warrant for Bang Si-hyuk, chairman and founder of HYBE. Korea JoongAng Daily reported that prosecutors said there was not enough basis at this stage to require detention and requested supplementary investigation. AP described the same decision as prosecutors questioning whether detention was necessary while Bang faces a high-profile investor-fraud investigation.

That distinction matters. A rejected warrant can sound, in fandom discourse, like a collapse of the case. Legally and reputationally, it is narrower than that. Prosecutors did not say the issue was imaginary. They declined to move forward with custody on the record submitted by police. For HYBE, that creates an uncomfortable middle state: the most dramatic immediate risk has eased, but the story is still alive enough to keep resurfacing whenever investors discuss governance.

### Police had moved only three days earlier

The speed of the reversal added to the noise. On `April 21`, the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency said it had applied for a warrant on suspicions tied to fraudulent or unfair trading. Korea Times, Korea JoongAng Daily and AP all aligned on the core frame: police allege that Bang misled investors in `2019`, before Big Hit Entertainment's public listing, by indicating there were no immediate plans to go public.

Bang's side denies wrongdoing. Korea Times reported that his side said the IPO followed the law and regulations, while Korea JoongAng Daily quoted his legal position as one of continued cooperation. That denial is not a footnote. It is central to how this story must be read. The case remains an allegation-driven investigation, not a conviction, and the public record is still being built.

## The $135M Number Is Why This Keeps Traveling

### The allegation is about the pre-IPO share path

The heart of the investigation is not a normal entertainment-company scandal. It is a capital-markets story. Police suspect that existing shareholders sold shares to a private equity fund after being told there were no plans for a public listing, before the company later proceeded toward its `2020` IPO. According to Korea JoongAng Daily, police suspect Bang received `30 percent` of post-listing resale profits under a prior agreement with that fund.

Different outlets have framed the suspected amount slightly differently. Korea JoongAng Daily put one key figure at about `190 billion won`, or roughly `$128 million`, while AP described police belief that the fund may have paid Bang around `200 billion won`, or about `$135 million`, through a side arrangement. Those numbers are why the story travels outside Korea. They make the dispute legible to global readers who may not follow Korean securities law but instantly understand the scale of a nine-figure allegation.

### The IPO context makes the claim sharper

Big Hit's listing was never ordinary. Korea JoongAng Daily reported in `2020` that the BTS label debuted on the Kospi at `270,000 won`, double its `135,000 won` offering price, then briefly rose to `351,000 won` before closing at `258,000 won`. That first-day volatility now matters in hindsight because the current allegations sit directly around the moment when private ownership, public valuation and investor expectations collided.

The public does not need to know every technical layer of an IPO to sense the tension. If a company tells one story before listing and the market sees a very different outcome after listing, regulators will care about who knew what, when they knew it and who profited from the gap. That is why this case is more serious than a celebrity-management controversy. It cuts into the financial architecture behind K-pop's biggest corporate success story.

## HYBE's Business Machine Is Still Running Hot

### The timing is brutally awkward

The warrant decision landed while HYBE was also trying to sell a much stronger business narrative. Music Business Worldwide reported that HYBE generated `698.3 billion won` in Q1 2026 revenue, up `39.5 percent` year over year and its highest-ever first-quarter total. Recorded-music revenue jumped `98.9 percent` to `271.5 billion won`, driven by BTS's `ARIRANG` comeback.

That should have been the clean headline: the flagship act is back, recorded music is surging and the global tour cycle is only beginning to flow through the accounts. Instead, HYBE now has two stories moving at once. One is about operational power. The other is about founder-level legal exposure. For a company whose valuation depends on trust in platforms, artists, fans and capital markets, those stories cannot stay neatly separated.

### The investigation also changes how success is interpreted

Strong revenue can calm investors, but it cannot answer a governance question by itself. In fact, success can make the question louder. If HYBE is entering one of the richest BTS-driven periods in its history, then founder conduct, disclosure norms and shareholder fairness become more important, not less. The bigger the machine gets, the less room there is for ambiguity around how that machine was built.

This is the part fans sometimes resist. Loving an artist does not require defending every corporate process around that artist. At the same time, criticizing a corporate investigation does not erase the scale of what HYBE has built. Both can be true: BTS can be commercially historic, and HYBE's chairman can still face a serious regulatory cloud.

## MEARROW's View On The HYBE Investor-Fraud Investigation

### The real risk is trust compression

MEARROW sees this as a trust-compression story. HYBE is being squeezed from three sides at once. Fans want the BTS era to feel celebratory. Investors want the Q1 rebound to signal durable growth. Regulators want clarity on whether pre-IPO shareholders were misled. None of those audiences reads the same headline the same way, but each one affects the others.

If prosecutors eventually decide there is no chargeable case, HYBE may frame the episode as an overextended investigation that failed to justify detention. If the case strengthens, every future earnings beat will carry a governance discount. That is the real danger. A company can survive one bad headline. It has a harder time thriving when every good headline arrives with a legal asterisk.

### The next signal matters more than the rejected warrant

The rejected arrest request is only the current checkpoint. The next meaningful signal will be whether police can supply the supplementary evidence prosecutors asked for, whether prosecutors move from detention questions to formal charges, and how clearly HYBE communicates with shareholders while the process continues.

For now, Bang Si-hyuk has not been arrested, and he denies wrongdoing. The case, however, did not disappear. It moved into a slower and more consequential phase: the one where K-pop's largest company has to prove that its governance can withstand the same global attention as its artists.
