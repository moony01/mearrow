In December 2023, the Incheon Metropolitan Police officially closed their investigation into G-Dragon (Kwon Ji-yong). No charges. No evidence. Case closed. Fans celebrated. The industry exhaled. At that point, the conventional K-pop playbook called for a quiet return — let the controversy fade, focus on music, move forward.

G-Dragon chose a different approach.

In February 2026, his agency Galaxy Corporation, working with top-tier law firm Yulchon, filed criminal complaints against more than 100 individuals for defamation, spreading false information, malicious slander, and violation of personal rights. Then on March 23, 2026, the scope widened. G-Dragon's legal team confirmed it had moved against not just individual commenters — but the **operators** of the online media outlets that originally published the allegations against him.

## What Was Published

The media outlet at the center of the expanded case reportedly published multiple articles based on claims about G-Dragon's presence at specific locations — framing them as circumstantial evidence of drug use. Those reports became the fuel for tens of thousands of malicious online comments and widespread secondary sharing.

Police found nothing to support the allegations. No testimony. No evidence. The case was closed without charges. But the articles stayed live. The comments stayed up. The reputational damage persisted.

## An Unprecedented Scale of Legal Response

K-pop artists suing over defamation isn't new. What G-Dragon's legal operation represents is different in both scale and strategy.

**100+ simultaneous complaints.** Most celebrity defamation cases in Korea target a small, identified group. Filing against 100+ individuals simultaneously sends a systemic message: every comment is traceable. No one is anonymous enough to be safe.

**Targeting media operators, not just commenters.** This is the escalation that matters most. Suing individual commenters targets the downstream damage. Suing the outlet operators who published the original allegations targets the source. It's an argument that platforms enabling false accusatory reporting bear legal liability for the consequences — a precedent that, if established, would reshape how Korean online entertainment media operates.

**Timing is deliberate.** This legal offensive is running in parallel with BIGBANG's 20th anniversary activities, Coachella 2026 preparation, and what is shaping up to be G-Dragon's most commercially active year since his return. The message is unmistakable: the legal war and the career comeback are happening simultaneously, not sequentially.

## "Some Suspects Have Already Admitted to the Charges"

Galaxy Corporation's public statement went further than a typical legal warning:

> "We are continuing strong legal action in cooperation with law firm Yulchon regarding malicious posts involving defamation, dissemination of false information, malicious slander, and violations of G-Dragon's personal rights. Some suspects have admitted to the charges, and questioning of those accused has been completed. The case has now been forwarded to the prosecutors, and we are awaiting their decision. Active investigations are also underway against suspects who deny the allegations."

Admissions on record. Cases already forwarded to prosecutors. This is not a threat. It is an active legal process with documented outcomes.

## Why Targeting Media Operators Changes the Equation

Korean online entertainment media has operated for years under a model where allegation-based reporting about celebrities carries minimal legal risk. An outlet publishes speculative content based on anonymous tips. If the allegations prove false, a retraction — if issued at all — generates a fraction of the original article's traffic. The reputational damage lingers long after the correction.

G-Dragon's lawyers are now arguing that operators of outlets who published false suspicion-based reporting on him should bear legal liability. This is not a common position in Korean media law, and the courts have not uniformly applied it. If successful, it sets a precedent with consequences far beyond this specific case: it raises the legal cost of publishing unverified allegations against public figures.

Whether the courts agree will determine whether this becomes a landmark case or an aggressive but ultimately contained legal action.

## The Comeback Happening Simultaneously

G-Dragon's legal campaign is running alongside arguably the most active period of his career since BIGBANG's pre-COVID years. His 'Übermensch' solo exhibition surpassed 250,000 visitors in the first half of 2026. BIGBANG's 20th anniversary world tour is confirmed. Coachella 2026 is on the schedule.

The juxtaposition is striking: an artist who spent nearly two years under the shadow of unproven drug allegations is simultaneously pursuing legal accountability for those who spread them and preparing to headline some of the world's largest music stages. The narrative — wrongly accused, cleared, and now fighting back — is structurally compelling in a way that a quiet return could never be.

## MEARROW's Take

This is being read in some quarters as personal revenge. It is also something else: the first large-scale test of whether the K-pop defamation ecosystem can be held legally accountable.

The industry has long operated with an implicit understanding that allegations against idols — however unverified — carry limited legal risk for those who spread them. The expected response from the accused party is damage control: issue a statement, go quiet, hope the news cycle moves on. Legal action is considered disproportionate or bad optics.

G-Dragon's approach challenges all three assumptions.

**The scale is systemic:** 100+ complaints cannot be dismissed as a targeted grudge. It signals that legal exposure for online defamation is real, trackable, and being pursued.

**The target expansion matters:** Moving from individual commenters to media operators is a structural argument about content liability. If courts accept it, the risk calculus for publishing unverified allegations changes permanently.

**Outcomes are being disclosed:** Announcing that suspects have admitted charges and that cases have been forwarded to prosecutors is not standard procedure. It's a public accountability record. It says: this is not theater. There are consequences.

G-Dragon didn't return quietly. What that costs him in terms of ongoing media friction — and what it gains the broader K-pop industry in terms of legal precedent — remains to be seen.

But one thing is already clear: the era of treating K-pop idol defamation as a cost-free sport may be coming to an end.
