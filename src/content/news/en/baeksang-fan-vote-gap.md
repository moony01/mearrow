Baeksang Popularity Award voting was supposed to be a side race before the serious trophies. Instead, it became the cleanest fan-power chart of the week. YoonA finished the female race with 58.1 percent, while Park Jihoon took the male side with 48.7 percent, according to reports citing Naver vote data after polling closed. The number is the hook, but the deeper story is not only who won. It is that two idols who moved into acting are still being carried by fandom systems that behave like organized campaign infrastructure. ## The Baeksang Popularity Award Became A Fandom Test

### The voting design rewarded daily organization

The 62nd Baeksang Arts Awards' Naver Popularity Award vote ran from noon on April 24 until 11:59 p.m. KST on May 5, according to Cineplay and BizEnter. The rules were simple but revealing: each Naver account could cast one free vote per day in the male category and one free vote per day in the female category. That meant the winner would not be decided by one viral night. It would be decided by whether fans could return, remind, coordinate, and repeat for 12 straight days.

That structure is familiar to K-pop. Music-show voting, streaming guides, birthday billboard campaigns, album-buying teams, and app-based popularity polls have trained fandoms to treat affection as schedule management. A casual supporter might vote once. A disciplined fandom turns voting into a calendar. That is why this Baeksang result matters for MEARROW: it shows the same operating logic escaping music and landing inside Korea's broader entertainment awards circuit.

### The nominee pool made the win harder to dismiss

This was not a tiny fan poll with only idol names on the board. Cineplay reported that the race covered 79 nominees across broadcasting, film, theater, and musical categories: 41 men and 38 women. The eventual popularity winners were selected from the candidates with the highest vote totals across those fields. In other words, idol-linked fandoms were not only competing with other idol fandoms. They were competing with drama, film, variety, and stage audiences at once.

That makes the margins harder to treat as noise. Koreaboo and Filmibeat both reported YoonA at 58.1 percent in the female category and Park Jihoon at 48.7 percent in the male category. Those are not symbolic leads. They are evidence of fan bases that could still mobilize outside a normal comeback cycle.

## YoonA And Park Jihoon Are Not Only Acting Wins

### Their idol histories changed the voting math

YoonA's number is especially telling because she is not a newcomer being discovered by award-season viewers. She is Girls' Generation's YoonA, a second-generation idol whose public identity has stretched across music, dramas, films, endorsements, and hosting for nearly two decades. When a figure like that enters a public-vote race, she does not start from a blank acting profile. She brings memory, nostalgia, fandom loyalty, and a multi-generation recognition base.

Park Jihoon's case works differently but points in the same direction. He is not only an actor from The King's Warden or Weak Hero discourse. He is also a former Wanna One member and solo artist, which means his public-vote base was built through survival-show fandom, idol promotion, and actor fandom layered on top of one another. Korea JoongAng Daily noted that The King's Warden led the Baeksang film field alongside No Other Choice with seven nominations, including a Rookie Award nomination for Park. The professional recognition may have raised his acting profile, but the popularity race shows how much his idol-era mobilization still matters.

## The Gap Says More Than The Trophy

### Popularity awards measure labor, not just affection

The easiest reading is that fans simply love YoonA and Park Jihoon. That is true, but incomplete. In public-vote systems, love only becomes visible when it is converted into repeated action. Someone has to circulate links, explain login rules, translate instructions, remind international fans about Korean time, and keep the campaign emotionally alive after the first burst fades.

That labor is why the percentages matter. A 58.1 percent female result and a 48.7 percent male result do not happen by passive admiration alone. They suggest disciplined voting behavior in a category where every day reset the field. For entertainment companies, this kind of proof is valuable because it confirms that an artist's fandom can still be activated even when the product is not an album, concert ticket, or comeback stage.

### The idol-to-actor path now carries measurable fandom equity

K-pop has always exported idols into acting, musicals, variety, fashion, and advertising. What has changed is the precision of the measurement. A role can be reviewed by critics, a drama can be measured by ratings, and a film can be tracked by admissions. But a daily public-vote race shows a different asset: how many people will perform small, repeated actions when an artist asks for no new music at all.

That is why idol agencies should care about Baeksang's popularity numbers. YoonA and Park Jihoon are not agency rookies chasing attention through a new single. They are proof that idol-origin fandom can survive career migration. If that fandom follows an artist into acting awards, it can also follow them into musicals, brand campaigns, paid fan meetings, streaming premieres, and global promotions.

## MEARROW's View On The Baeksang Fan Vote Gap

### Fandom infrastructure is becoming portable

MEARROW reads this result as a portability story. In older entertainment logic, a singer's fandom, an actor's audience, and an award show's viewers were treated as separate groups. The Baeksang vote shows that those borders are weaker than they look. A K-pop fandom trained inside voting apps can move into a prestige awards context and immediately understand the mechanics.

That creates a business advantage. Artists with active fandom infrastructure can bring attention to platforms, broadcasters, sponsors, and ceremonies that are not strictly music products. For Baeksang, idol-linked voters create traffic and social buzz before the ceremony. For the artists, the award becomes another public proof of relevance. For agencies, it is a reminder that fan relationship management cannot stop when an idol steps into acting.

### The next question is credibility

There is a tension here. Popularity awards are meant to celebrate public affection, but when fandom organization becomes too efficient, casual viewers can start reading the result as a machine instead of a mood. That does not make the result fake. It means the award is measuring a very specific kind of popularity: not broad passive awareness, but organized active support.

That distinction should be stated clearly. YoonA and Park Jihoon's reported leads do not prove they were the best performers of the year. Baeksang's judged categories exist for that. What the vote proves is different and still powerful: when fans were asked to show up every day, their fandoms did.

## The Signal Before The Ceremony

### The red carpet now starts online

The 62nd Baeksang Arts Awards are scheduled for May 8 at COEX D Hall in Seoul, with Shin Dong-yup, Suzy, and Park Bo-gum hosting. By the time the ceremony begins, the popularity story has already done its job. It pulled fans into the awards conversation before any winner walked onstage.

That may be the real lesson. Award shows no longer begin when the broadcast starts. They begin when voting links open, screenshots spread, fandom accounts publish reminders, and percentages turn into arguments. YoonA and Park Jihoon did not only win a popularity race. They showed that in 2026, K-pop fandom can enter a prestige awards system and make the numbers move before the lights come up.
