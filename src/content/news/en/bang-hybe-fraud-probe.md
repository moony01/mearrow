No arrest is the headline. More pressure is the story.

On April 24, 2026, South Korean prosecutors rejected police's request for an arrest warrant against HYBE chairman Bang Si-hyuk in the company's long-running IPO-related fraud case. That sounds, at first glance, like a break for HYBE. It is not. The prosecutors did not clear Bang. They sent the filing back and told police to strengthen the case. In other words, the `Bang Si-hyuk arrest warrant` story did not end. The `HYBE fraud probe` simply moved into a more dangerous phase: one where the company cannot rely on a single procedural headline to calm investors, fans, or business partners. ## What prosecutors actually did

### A rejected warrant is not an acquittal

According to AP, the Seoul Southern District Prosecutors' Office rejected the police request on Friday, April 24, saying the case did not yet show enough grounds to justify detention. AJU Press reported the same procedural point in slightly sharper language: prosecutors returned the warrant request and asked police for additional investigation. That distinction matters. Prosecutors did not say the allegations were weak enough to dismiss. They said the detention logic was not sufficiently explained at this stage.

### Police are still pursuing the same core allegation

The underlying accusation did not change. Yonhap, AP, and AJU all reported that police believe Bang misled investors in 2019 by signaling that HYBE, then Big Hit Entertainment, had no immediate IPO plan or that the listing would be delayed. Investigators say those investors were pushed toward selling their shares to a private equity-linked structure before HYBE went public in October 2020. Police also suspect that Bang later received a portion of the gains through a prearranged profit-sharing agreement. The case is still about whether that sequence amounted to fraudulent unfair trading under Korea's Capital Markets Act, not about whether this week's arrest paperwork was successful.

## The 200 billion won question is still the center of gravity

### Why the money number keeps following this case

The reason this story keeps detonating in headlines is simple: the alleged profit figure is enormous. AP put the suspected side deal at around 200 billion won, or roughly $135 million. Yonhap cited nearly 200 billion won in alleged illegal profit. AJU reported a police estimate of about 190 billion won and said some reports put the potential gain as high as 260 billion won. The exact final figure is still contested, but the order of magnitude is clear enough to explain why this is no longer a niche governance story for Korean finance reporters.

### The alleged 30 percent side arrangement changes the optics

Police also believe the private equity fund may have promised Bang about 30 percent of post-IPO stock-sale profits. If that allegation is eventually substantiated, the reputational damage would not come only from the amount involved. It would come from the structure. The optics of a founder privately benefiting from a sequence that early investors say they did not fully understand is exactly the kind of narrative that public-company markets punish long before any court reaches a final conclusion.

## Why HYBE still has a narrative problem

### BTS momentum cannot fully shield a listed company

This is the uncomfortable timing problem for HYBE. AP noted that Bang's legal troubles have become a public-relations setback just as BTS resumes major tour activity after military service. The group has already performed comeback shows in Seoul, Goyang, and Tokyo, with U.S. dates about to begin. In entertainment terms, this should have been a clean stretch of recovery and brand expansion. Instead, the founder's legal exposure keeps dragging the frame back to internal controls, disclosure standards, and executive accountability.

### A procedural win can still make the story bigger

There is a reason procedural reversals sometimes intensify coverage rather than calm it. A denied warrant does not create closure. It creates suspense. Investors now have to watch whether police can rebuild the filing, whether prosecutors' concerns were technical or substantive, and whether HYBE can separate Bang's personal legal defense from the institutional credibility of the company. That is not a resolved story. It is a story that became harder to summarize in one line.

## How the public is reading Bang right now

### Bang's name now carries two conflicting narratives

Bang still occupies a rare place in K-pop's public imagination: he is both the founder most closely associated with BTS's global rise and the executive now repeatedly named in a capital-markets probe. Recent coverage shows those two narratives colliding. On one side, HYBE still benefits from the scale of BTS's return. On the other, AP's framing of the case as a public-relations setback makes clear that the mainstream reading of Bang is no longer just "hitmaker" or "builder." It is "builder under scrutiny."

### Public perception and historical fact are different layers

That shift in public mood matters because it changes how every new procedural update is received. It does not, however, decide whether the 2019 allegations are true. Current distrust, fatigue, or fandom loyalty cannot substitute for evidence. The public may read the rejected warrant as proof that the case is collapsing, or as proof that bigger evidence is coming. Both reactions can coexist. Neither establishes the historical facts on its own.

## MEARROW's view on what became more dangerous

### HYBE is now being tested as a system, not as a fandom machine

The most important change after April 24 is not legal. It is strategic. Before this rejection, HYBE could treat the warrant request as a dramatic spike in attention. After the rejection, the company is stuck in a slower, more corrosive zone where every update will be interpreted through governance risk. That is harder to manage than a single shock headline. It forces a public company to show process, transparency, and institutional depth.

### The founder question is now the company question

HYBE's scale was built on artist power, fan mobilization, and founder authority operating together. Cases like this separate those elements. BTS can still sell out shows. HYBE artists can still stream at massive scale. None of that answers whether the company has persuaded the market that its governance architecture can stand apart from Bang's personal legal exposure. That is why the rejected warrant may be more dangerous than the original filing. It prolongs the question instead of resolving it.

## What changes now

### Watch the next filing, not the relief cycle

The instinctive reaction to Friday's update is relief. The better reaction is attention. Police can supplement the case and return. Prosecutors can reassess. HYBE can either communicate like a mature public company or hide behind the size of its artist machine and hope the tour cycle overwhelms the headlines. The next chapter will decide which reading was right. For now, the cleanest conclusion is also the least comfortable one: Bang Si-hyuk avoided arrest this week, but HYBE did not escape the probe that now defines its most sensitive moment.
