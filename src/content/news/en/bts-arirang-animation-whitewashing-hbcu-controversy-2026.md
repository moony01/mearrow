One animated trailer. Seven days of controversy. And the biggest K-pop album in years drops tomorrow.

On March 13, BTS released the **ARIRANG Animation Trailer: "What is your love song?"** — a beautifully crafted 2-minute-49-second short film tracing seven Korean students' historic 1896 visit to Howard University in Washington D.C. Within days, the response had split in two: awe at the historical depth, and anger at what critics called a glaring omission. **The Black students of Howard University — the reason Howard exists — were nearly invisible in the animation.**

## What The Trailer Shows

### The 1896 Setting

The ARIRANG animation is rooted in a real historical event. In May 1896, The Washington Post documented seven young Korean (then Joseon) students visiting Howard University, where they performed for the student body. That July, some of those students made wax cylinder recordings — believed to be the **first-known audio recording of Arirang**, and among the earliest documented recordings of Korean voices in American history.

HYBE explicitly acknowledged this source: the trailer's opening card reads:

> *"This video was inspired by the story of seven young Koreans as documented in The Washington Post on May 8, 1896 ('Seven Koreans at Howard'), some of whom captured the first known audio recordings of Koreans in Washington, D.C., on July 24."*

The animation is beautifully produced, drawing a red thread between 1896 and 2026, connecting seven young Koreans then to seven BTS members now.

### What Critics Noticed

The problem critics identified: **Howard University in 1896 was predominantly Black.** Founded in 1867 specifically to provide education for Black Americans following the Civil War, Howard has been called the "Mecca" of Black higher education. In 1896, the overwhelming majority of its students — the very audience that welcomed those seven Korean students — would have been Black.

In the animation, **nearly all bystanders, students, and crowd members in the Howard University scenes appear to be white or light-skinned**. Critics counted only one or two visibly Black characters in crowd scenes.

## The Internet Reacts

### The Backlash

Posts surfaced first on Korean online communities before spreading rapidly to English-language K-pop Twitter (X), TikTok, and Reddit. The criticisms clustered around two arguments:

**"This is erasure."** Howard University in 1896 was a Black institution. Depicting its student body as predominantly white removes the Black students from their own history. The Korean visitors were welcomed by Black students — that context is not incidental. It is the entire point. The erasure of that context, critics argued, diminishes the significance of what took place.

**"HYBE should have known better."** Given that ARIRANG is built on a story of cultural preservation — Korean identity surviving through colonial threat — critics found the parallel especially painful. A story about not being erased was, they argued, erasing someone else.

"It's disrespectful," read one widely circulated post. "You can't tell a story about seven Koreans at Howard University in 1896 and make the background almost entirely white."

### The Defense

ARMY pushback was swift and pointed to several factors:

**The disclaimer.** HYBE included explicit language that the animation was "inspired by" the historical event — not a documentary recreation. The trailer itself opens with that caveat.

**Historical nuance.** White faculty and administrators were present at Howard in 1896, including Alice Cunningham Fletcher, a white ethnomusicologist who worked with the Library of Congress and was involved in some of the wax cylinder recording sessions. The animation's white-presenting characters are not historically impossible.

**Artistic license.** Animated historical pieces routinely simplify and stylize. The argument that every scene must be a precise demographic representation applies a standard rarely enforced elsewhere.

**The larger intent.** The animation, defenders argued, was clearly made in good faith to honor a story of cultural connection — not to diminish anyone.

## Why Howard University Makes This Uniquely Charged

### The HBCU Context

This controversy doesn't exist in a vacuum. It sits inside a specific American cultural conversation about **the erasure of Black historical spaces**.

Howard University is not simply a university that happens to be majority Black. It is an institution that **exists because Black Americans were excluded from every other option**. In 1867, Black students had no access to the educational infrastructure that white students took for granted. Howard was built to fill that gap — and its history is inseparable from that founding purpose.

When those seven Korean students arrived in 1896, they were stepping into one of the only spaces in America where their presence would be welcomed without the racial hierarchy that defined nearly everything else in late-19th-century Washington D.C. The Black students who greeted them were not a neutral backdrop. They were the hosts, in their own house.

To animate that scene with a predominantly white crowd is not just historically imprecise. For many viewers, it fundamentally misreads the room.

### The K-pop Industry's Recurring Race Problem

This controversy is not isolated. The K-pop industry has faced repeated criticism over its relationship with Black American culture and its depiction of race more broadly:

- Multiple groups have faced backlash for using Black cultural elements (braids, AAVE, hip-hop aesthetics) without acknowledgment or credit
- Skin whitening practices and "whitewashing" in idol promotion is a documented, ongoing tension
- Several labels have issued apologies for promotional materials that critics deemed racially insensitive

In each case, the charge is similar: the industry benefits from Black culture while failing to see — or center — the Black people who created it.

## HYBE's Silence

As of March 19 — **one day before ARIRANG releases** — HYBE has issued no statement specifically addressing the whitewashing accusations. The animation remains live on YouTube without modification or additional context.

This silence follows a pattern. Korean entertainment companies generally avoid engaging with controversy unless fan pressure or commercial risk forces a response. Given the scale of tomorrow's launch, any response now would be awkward: too little, too late before release; potentially too damaging to address in the launch window.

The calculation HYBE appears to be making: the core fanbase is mobilized, the controversy has not crossed into mainstream media coverage, and the album speaks for itself. Whether that calculation holds depends on whether the whitewashing story gets picked up beyond K-pop Twitter.

## MEARROW's Take: The Contradiction At The Heart Of ARIRANG

Here is what makes this controversy genuinely painful rather than merely complicated: **ARIRANG's entire conceptual premise is about not being erased.**

The 1896 wax cylinder recordings exist because seven young Koreans, standing on the eve of colonial occupation, sang into a machine to prove they existed. The album title, the animation, the red thread — all of it points to the same idea: *we were here, we sang, we survived.*

That story is profound. The resonance it creates with BTS's own history — a group that went silent for three years and returned — is real.

But the story of those seven Koreans at Howard is not a story that happened in a vacuum. It happened in a Black institution, among Black students who were themselves fighting not to be erased from American life. To animate that story while rendering those Black students nearly invisible is, at minimum, a failure of historical imagination. At worst, it reproduces the very erasure the album is meant to protest.

HYBE may have had every good intention. The animation may be artistically beautiful. The album — which drops tomorrow — may be extraordinary.

None of that resolves the contradiction.

On the eve of what may be the most significant K-pop event in years, ARMY is being asked to hold two things simultaneously: genuine admiration for a deeply researched historical concept, and genuine discomfort with how that history was drawn. That is not a comfortable position. But it may be the most honest one available.

Tomorrow, March 20, ARIRANG arrives. The conversation about how it got here — and who was visible in the telling — will outlast the release week.
